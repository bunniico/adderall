# How the app plans your days

When the app picks a deadline or a slot for you, it puts it on a day that can hold it.

## How it works

The app keeps a book of what each day already holds, across every project: deadlines you set, work it has already placed, and the future copies of [repeating tasks](19-repeating-tasks.md). Each new task gets the first slot that fits, laid out through your working day and rolling onto the next day when one is full.

When two tasks want the same day and it cannot hold both, the one with the higher [score](14-the-score.md) keeps it and the other moves to the next day with room.

Your working day is set in ⚙ Settings → *How much a day holds*:

- **Hours of work in a day**: how much a day is allowed to hold (8 by default).
- **Learn my real day**: lets that number move to match the days you actually have.
- **Which days you work**: nothing the app schedules for itself lands on a day you have not ticked.
- **Day start and end**: nothing the app schedules goes before or after these.
- **Spread work out**: gives each task its own slot on a day with room, rather than stacking them.

The calendar warns when a day is booked past its cap, and says which number it is using and why.

## Why it works this way

Fifteen things braindumped in one minute used to come back as fifteen blocks stacked on the same afternoon. That is a plan you bounce off rather than start.

**It only looks forward.** Nothing is quietly pulled earlier, and nothing already overdue is quietly moved out of the red. A whole task tree is placed as one span, so a project's steps fill the block their parent occupies.

**Your own dates win.** A deadline or start time you set yourself is not moved by the cap, and neither is a repeating job whose own rule names that day. You still have to eat dinner on a day that is already full; the calendar saying the day is overbooked is the honest answer.

**The day cap learns** because eight hours is the number most likely to be wrong for any particular person. Clear it every day and it rises; never reach it and it falls halfway toward the day you really have. Only days you finished something on count, so a weekend off is not evidence. A goal you have never hit is not a plan; it is a daily notice that you failed.

When nothing has room before the horizon, the overflow goes to the emptiest day there is, rather than piling onto one afternoon.
